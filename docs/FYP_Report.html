Abstract

The authentication of a hadith — a reported saying of the Prophet or an Imam — rests on the credibility of the chain of people who transmitted it. That chain, the سند (sanad), is recorded in the hadith books; the credibility of each transmitter is recorded separately, in biographical dictionaries known as رجال (rijal) literature. Linking the two is manual work, and the volume makes it error-prone: a single collection runs to thousands of narrations naming tens of thousands of narrator mentions.

Zaraket and Makhlouta (2012) proposed ANGE, a system that extracts a narrator graph from hadith books using morphological analysis, finite state machines and graph transformations, then uses that graph to disambiguate and segment the biography books — a cross-document technique that raised narrator-boundary recall from 41% to 93%. Their implementation was written in C++/Qt against Sarf, an in-house morphological analyser backed by a MySQL lexicon.

This project reimplements ANGE in Python, replacing the Sarf and MySQL stack with CAMeL Tools for morphological disambiguation and Wojood for named-entity recognition. The work is not a translation. Reading the original implementation alongside the paper revealed that three mechanisms the paper describes were never implemented in the released code, and that two further routines contain defects that silently discard results. This report documents the reimplemented system in full — every stage from raw hadith text to an annotated biography — states each divergence from the original and its justification, and specifies the evaluation framework through which results will be reported once gold annotations are available.

The delivered system comprises 24 modules and a regression suite of 165 tests, none of which require the morphological models to be installed, so that the automaton and graph logic remain verifiable independently of the NLP layer.

1. Introduction

1.1 The problem

A hadith has two parts. The matn (متن) is the content — what was said. The sanad (سند) is the chain of transmission that precedes it: a list of people, each naming the person they heard it from, running back from the compiler of the book to the original speaker. Jurisprudence in every Islamic school of thought turns on whether that chain is sound, and a chain is sound only if each person in it is known and considered reliable.

Judging reliability requires a second body of literature. The rijal dictionaries devote an entry to each known narrator: who they studied under, who transmitted from them, where they lived, and what earlier scholars said about their trustworthiness. A scholar authenticating a chain must therefore, for every name in it, locate the corresponding entry in a rijal book — across works that run to several volumes each.

Two things make this hard to automate. First, neither corpus is segmented: a hadith book is a continuous stream of text in which the boundary between one narration and the next, and between sanad and matn, is not marked. Second, Arabic names are not identifiers. The same person appears as عبد الله بن عمر, as ابن عمر, and as عبد الله بن عمر رضي الله عنه; two different people may share every written component of their name and be distinguishable only by who they narrated from.

1.2 The approach

ANGE's insight is that the two corpora help each other. The hadith books, though harder to read individually, are highly repetitive: the same narrator appears in hundreds of chains alongside the same neighbours. Aggregating those chains produces a graph in which each person is a node and each transmission an edge. That graph then acts as external knowledge when reading the biography books: a name in a rijal entry can be checked against the graph, and — more importantly — the narrators named around it can be checked for whether the graph says they belong together.

The consequence, which the original paper reports and which this project reproduces structurally, is counter-intuitive. Using the graph makes the system detect fewer narrators in the biography books, because it keeps only those it can confirm. But the ones it keeps have correct boundaries, and boundary recall more than doubles.

1.3 Objectives

  1. Reimplement ANGE end to end in Python, replacing Sarf and MySQL with CAMeL Tools and Wojood, and verify the reimplementation against the original source rather than against the paper's prose alone.
  2. Implement the evaluation framework the original used, so that results are comparable in kind to the published ones.
  3. Identify and document every point at which the released implementation diverges from the published description, and justify each choice made in response.
  4. Produce a system whose logic is testable without the heavyweight NLP models, so that regressions surface immediately rather than after a multi-hour run.

1.4 Contributions

Beyond the reimplementation itself, this project makes four contributions that were not available before it.

Three unimplemented claims implemented. Reading the C++ source line by line established that three mechanisms described in the paper are absent from the released system: the conflicting/reinforcing nisba logic in the name-distance metric, a tunable k in the k-reachable neighbourhood search, and the sequential ambiguity constraint in biography linking. Section 3.3 gives the evidence. All three are implemented here, kept switchable, and reported separately from the faithful baseline.

Two defects corrected. The candidate-ranking routine updateGroups discards the better of two competing results rather than the worse; the evaluation scorer omits unmatched gold items from its denominator, inflating recall. Both are analysed in Section 3.4.

A verifiable test suite. 165 regression tests, none of which require CAMeL Tools or Wojood, covering the automaton, the graph algorithms, the scorers and the file formats.

A gold-annotation layer that reads the original binaries. The original gold files were excluded from the published repository. A reader for the Qt QDataStream format used by those files is provided, so that if the originals are recovered they can be scored directly.

Three features of this example recur throughout and shape the design.

The chain is joined by narration connectors — here عن ("from"), elsewhere حدثنا ("narrated to us") and its morphological variants. These are the strongest signal available that a narrator boundary has occurred, and the finite state machine of Chapter 5 is built around them.

A narrator's name is itself structured. أحمد بن إدريس is two name components joined by بن ("son of"), which establishes parenthood and therefore generation. Chapter 6 shows how this structure is exploited to compare names.

Not every element of a chain is a person. بعض أصحابنا ("some of our companions") is a collective placeholder, and عليه السلام ("peace be upon him") is an honorific attached to the preceding name, not a narrator in its own right. Section 5.5 describes how the system distinguishes these, and why failing to do so was the single largest source of error in an earlier iteration.

The teacher and student names are the bridge between the two corpora. If the graph built from the hadith books says that إبراهيم الشيباني transmitted from أبي الجارود and to حرب بن الحسين, and a stretch of the rijal book mentions all three in close proximity, that stretch is very likely the entry for إبراهيم الشيباني — and its extent can be inferred from where those confirmed names begin and end.

It should be said plainly that entries are not always this tidy. Many carry full isnads inline, quoting other scholars at length; many are cross-references of the form "X: see Y"; and the numbering restarts across sections. Section 7.6 returns to this.

2.3 Why Arabic morphology matters here

Arabic words are built from a stem with prefixes and suffixes attached orthographically, without spaces. The single token وحدثنا is the conjunction و ("and") plus the stem حدث ("narrated") plus the suffix نا ("to us"). A system that matches surface strings against a list of narration connectors will miss it; a system that works on morphological analyses will not.

Three specific consequences run through this project.

Connectors must be recognised through their morphology. The lexicons in this system are consulted with both the surface form and the lemma supplied by the morphological analyser, so that وعن matches the narration-connector list through its lemma عن.

Orthographic variation must be normalised before comparison. The several forms of alef (أ إ آ ا ى) are used inconsistently across editions, as are ة/ه and the presence of diacritics. Section 5.1 describes the normalisation applied, and the index mapping that allows offsets in the normalised text to be traced back to the original.

A conjunction attached to a name splits narrators. In سهل بن زياد وإسحاق بن محمد the و prefixed to إسحاق marks a second, parallel narrator. Detecting that prefix — by observing that the lemma lacks the و that the surface form carries — is what prevents the two from being merged into one impossible name.

2.4 The morphological stack: from Sarf to CAMeL and Wojood

The original system used Sarf, an in-house analyser developed in the same research group, backed by a MySQL database of stems, affixes and category annotations. Sarf returned, for each word, the set of possible analyses together with abstract category bits — Male Names, Compound Names, Name of Place, City/Town, POSSESSIVE — and gloss descriptions in English that the system matched against ("son", "father", "narrate", "listen").

That stack is not practically available today: it requires the database, the build environment and the Qt runtime. This project replaces it with two components that are maintained and installable.

The substitution is not neutral, and Section 5.4 documents the one place where it caused a substantive problem: CAMeL's noun_prop tag is applied far more freely than Sarf's name categories were, and accepting it uncritically caused ordinary words in the matn to be read as narrators.

A note on what is and is not being claimed. Replacing the morphological layer changes what the system sees, so the numbers this system produces are not directly comparable with the published ones even on the same books. What is comparable is the shape of the experiment: the same metric definitions, the same two conditions, the same ablations. Chapter 10 is written to make that distinction explicit rather than to obscure it.

3. The Original System and What Reading It Revealed

3.1 ANGE as described in the paper

ANGE proceeds in four stages. A hadith book is segmented into narrations by a finite state machine driven by morphological features. The narrator sequences so obtained are merged into a graph, using a hierarchical name-distance metric and a multi-key hash to keep the comparison tractable. The same machinery is then run over a biography book, with the graph supplying external evidence. Finally, the output is scored against human annotation.

The paper reports three result tables: narrator-graph accuracy (Table 1), narrator detection and boundary accuracy in biographies with and without the graph (Table 2), and biography detection (Table 3), plus a separate figure for partial graph annotation — 80% recall, 89% precision — in §4.1.

The released implementation is roughly 10,000 lines of C++ in src/case/, built on Qt. The correspondence between the paper and the code is close in the main, and the port in Chapters 5 to 8 follows it module for module. Table 3.1 gives the mapping.

3.3 Three mechanisms described but not implemented

3.3.1 The nisba logic in the distance metric

Section 3.2 of the paper describes a distance function that reasons about the qualifying components of a name: it returns infinity when two qualifiers conflict (العراقي, the Iraqi, against المصري, the Egyptian) and rewards qualifiers that reinforce one another (العراقي with الكوفي, Kufa being a city in Iraq).

That function exists in the source, as getdistance at narrator_abstraction.cpp:399, complete with the geographic reasoning and the city and country lookups. It is never called; a search of the whole source tree finds no call site. The function actually used is equalNew at line 602, reached through equal() at line 672, and it ends:

//till now possessives not even compared
return (double)levelsEqual / levelMax;

The metric behind the published numbers is therefore a plain ratio of matching name levels, with qualifiers ignored.

3.3.2 A tunable k in the neighbourhood search

The paper lists k-reachable among its graph algorithms and uses it in the biography stage. The parameter exists — bio_max_reachability is declared in hadithCommon.h:37, defaulted, and wired to a text box in the graphical interface at biographies.h:132 — but it is never read. The neighbourhood search hardcodes a depth of one, in both directions:

node->BFS_traverse(cont, 1,  1);   // one hop, toward children
node->BFS_traverse(cont, 1, -1);   // one hop, toward parents

The operational value of k was therefore always 1.

3.3.3 The sequential ambiguity constraint

Section 4 of the paper states two measures for resolving an ambiguous name in a biography: thresholding on the hash score, and restricting the matches of narrator ni to those within k steps of the matches of ni−1. The first is implemented, as LocateInGraphAction keeping the highest-similarity nodes. The second appears nowhere in narratordetector.cpp; the linking path makes no reference to the preceding narrator at all.

3.4 Two defects in the released code

3.4.1 updateGroups discards the better candidate

This routine (narratordetector.cpp:158) maintains the top three candidate biographies for a narrator:

bool add = false;
for (int i = 0; i < groups.size(); i++) {
    if (groups[i].score < set.score) { add = true; }
    else if (add) { groups.insert(i, set); break; }
}
if (groups.size() > MAX_SIZE) { groups.removeLast(); }
else if (!add && groups.size() < MAX_SIZE) { groups.append(set); return true; }
return add;

Trace it on a list held in descending order. If the incoming set scores higher than the first element, add is set on the first iteration. On the next iteration the same comparison holds — the list is descending — so add is set again and the else if branch is never reached. The loop ends with nothing inserted; the size check then fails, the !add branch is skipped, and the function returns having discarded the set. Only a set scoring lower than everything already present reaches the append.

The routine therefore retains its worst candidates and drops its best. This reimplementation implements the evident intention — retain the highest-scoring N — and records the divergence.

3.4.2 Dead code in the boundary path

getBoundingParagraph and isInsideBound (narratordetector.cpp:213–266) are defined but never called. They would have constrained a detected biography to lie within a paragraph. The paragraph delimiters that additionalCheck collects at line 378 are consequently gathered and never used. This system still collects them, since they are useful for inspection, but does not invent a use for them.

4. System Architecture

4.1 Overview

4.2 Design decisions

Offsets, not copies. Every structure stores character offsets into the normalised text rather than copies of it, as the original did with its m_start and m_end fields. The text exists once. This is what makes span-level agreement scoring possible at all, and it is why the normalisation step returns an index map back into the original document.

Subclassing rather than mode flags. The original threads a hadith boolean through every branch of a 500-line state function. Here the biography automaton is a subclass overriding the five behaviours that genuinely differ (Section 7.2), so the hadith path is provably unaffected by work on the biography side.

A separable NLP layer. Nothing below engine.py imports CAMeL or Wojood. Token features are a plain dataclass, so the automaton and the graph algorithms can be exercised with hand-constructed features. All 165 tests run without the models installed — a deliberate choice, since it means a regression surfaces in seconds rather than after a multi-hour book run.

Faithful and improved variants are switchable. Wherever this system departs from the original, the departure is a parameter and the faithful behaviour remains reachable. Section 9.3 lists the switches.

5. Stage 1 — From Book to Chains

5.1 Normalisation

Arabic editions vary orthographically in ways that defeat string comparison. Normalisation removes diacritics, tatweel and zero-width joiners; unifies the alef forms and the hamza carriers; maps ة to ه; turns stray symbols into spaces; and collapses runs of whitespace.

Because everything downstream is offsets, the function returns both the cleaned text and an index map: entry i of the map gives the position in the original document of character i of the cleaned text. Any span the system reports can therefore be shown to a reader in the original edition, diacritics and all.

5.4 Gating the name evidence

This section documents the most consequential correction made during the port. The reasoning is worth setting out, because the failure was not obvious from the output.

An early version accepted CAMeL's noun_prop tag as sufficient evidence that a token was a name, wherever it occurred. CAMeL applies that tag generously. The result was that ordinary words in the matn — الله, العقل — were read as narrators; three in a row satisfied narr_min; and the automaton emitted a sanad that did not exist. On the first volume of al-Kafi this produced 436 narrations that could not be matched to a hadith number, of which roughly 187 were pure matn.

The original does not trust its equivalent category either. The bit for the weak name category is deliberately excluded from the set of accepted name categories (hadithCommon.cpp:117–133) and admitted only while a flag named tryToLearnNames is set — that is, only where a name is already expected. Four such positions exist:

  1. the current word is a family connector (cpp:1160);
  2. the automaton is just past a narration word, at most one connector deep, and that connector was not merely punctuation (cpp:1172);
  3. the previous word was a narration word (cpp:1178);
  4. the word has the form of a nisba — beginning ال and ending ي — in a narration or name context (cpp:1227).

Two further filters apply in every case: the word must carry no suffix, and its stem must be at least three characters long.

This system reproduces that gate. A bare noun_prop now sets a candidate flag rather than is_name; a later pass promotes candidates in the four contexts above, subject to the same two filters, and records the promotion in the token's provenance list so that it remains visible in the output. Strong evidence — a Wojood PERS span, a known compound name — is accepted without gating, as before.

5.5 Honorifics are not narrators

The second substantive correction concerns عليه السلام.

The original keeps two lexicon files. src/case/stop_words lists references to the Prophet himself — رسول الله, الرسول, النبي — and these both end the sanad and mark the narrator. src/case/phrases lists honorific expressions — عليه السلام, رضي الله عنه — which are treated as compound units and are not narrators.

An early version of this system merged the two lists. Because Shia sanads terminate at an Imam rather than at the Prophet, treating عليه السلام as a sanad terminator is a reasonable adaptation to this corpus; the error lay in also letting it become a person. Measured on the first volume of al-Kafi: of 8,986 narrator positions, 1,031 — 11.5% — consisted of the bare honorific. It became the largest node in the narrator graph, with 1,116 occurrences and 143 outgoing edges, and a significant source of the impossible cycles discussed in Section 6.6.

The rule now implemented is that an honorific is never a narrator. A reference to the Prophet closes any open name and opens his node; a bare honorific contributes nothing and does not set the rasoul flag. The second point matters more than it appears, because the rasoul flag collapses every flagged narrator into a single graph node: marking أبي عبد الله عليه السلام as rasoul would fuse Imam al-Sadiq with Imam al-Baqir.

The honorific is also excluded from the stored name. Appending it would change the name's canonical key, so that أبي عبد الله عليه السلام and أبي عبد الله would fall into different hash buckets and never be compared — trading a visible error for a silent one.

5.6 Output of the stage

The stage writes one JSON file per book: for each accepted hadith, its number, the kitab and bab headings in force at that point, the narrators with their offsets, and the matn span. A readable text dump is written alongside for inspection, as the original wrote hadith_chains.txt.

A separate quality filter then removes narrations showing no structural evidence of being a sanad — no بن anywhere, no honorific ending, no narration connector between narrators. On the first volume this removed 186 of 1,740 candidates while dropping none of the 1,304 that carried a verified hadith number.

6. Stage 2 — The Narrator Graph

Stage 1 yields a list of chains. Each chain names people, but the same person appears in hundreds of chains, written differently each time. This stage decides which occurrences are the same human being, and connects them.

6.1 Canonicalising a name

An Arabic narrator name is not a flat string. It is a sequence of name components separated by بن ("son of"), which establishes descent, with qualifying components — a nisba of origin such as الكوفي — attached at the end. Canonicalisation splits the name into levels, one per generation.

NameCanonical form
عمارة بن القعقاع بن شبرمة [ عمارة ] | [ القعقاع ] | [ شبرمة ]
محمد بن يعقوب الكليني [ محمد ] | [ يعقوب ]   { الكليني }
أبو جعفر [ <AB> + جعفر ]
ابن أبي عمير [   ] | [ <AB> + عمير ]

Figure 6.1 — Canonical forms. Levels are separated by |; qualifiers appear in braces. The last row has an empty first level, because the person's own given name is unknown — the name begins with "son of".

Three details in this figure carry weight later.

Kunya connectors stay in the level. أبو and أم are not discarded; they occupy a slot in the level and are written as the tokens AB and OM when a key is built. This collapses the case variants أبو, أبي, أبا onto one token while keeping أبو جعفر distinct from a person simply named جعفر. An early version of this port discarded them, on the strength of a code comment claiming the original did so; reading isFamilyConnector() at narrator_abstraction.h:152 showed that it returns true for the kunya connectors as well, and that they are appended into the level. With them discarded, أبو جعفر scored a perfect match against جعفر.

A leading empty level is meaningful. It records that the first name is unknown. The original encodes this as a leading hyphen in the key and uses it to skip such names when choosing the subject of a biography entry, since a name that does not identify its bearer cannot identify an entry either.

Each level slot remembers whether it is a name or a connector. The comparison in the next section needs that distinction, and it cannot be recovered from the strings alone.

6.2 The distance metric

Two canonical forms are compared level by level, up to the length of the shorter. Within a level, slots are compared pairwise:

  • if both slots are connectors, the pair is skipped — this is what allows أبو جعفر and أبي جعفر to match;
  • if the slots differ in kind, or the words differ after alef normalisation, the comparison rejects outright and the score is zero;
  • otherwise the level counts as agreeing.

The score is the number of agreeing levels divided by the number of levels in the longer name. The hard rejection is the important half: it is what the paper means by returning infinity when components conflict at the same level.

The last two rows are the paper's argument in miniature. Edit distance rates خالد بن سعيد and خالد بن سعد at 0.92 — they differ by one letter — while the structural metric rejects them, because the grandfather level conflicts. Conversely edit distance rates a name and the same name with a nisba at only 0.62, because the added word is long, while the structural metric sees them as identical.

The metric also has a visible weakness, and the evaluation is designed to expose rather than hide it. A two-level name scores 0.67 against a three-level name sharing its prefix, so أحمد بن محمد matches both أحمد بن محمد بن عيسى and أحمد بن محمد بن خالد — who are different people. Section 11.1 returns to this.

6.3 The multi-key hash

Comparing every occurrence with every other is quadratic. On the corpus used here that is roughly nine thousand occurrences, so on the order of forty million comparisons for a single volume, and the full corpus is several times larger. The original's solution, reproduced here, is to index each name under several keys — one for each sub-name that could plausibly refer to the same person — and compare only names that share a key.

Two occurrences that share any key become candidates; the metric of Section 6.2 then decides. Because a short form such as عمارة generates a key that the full form also generates, an abbreviated mention still finds its fuller counterpart.

6.4 Merging

The construction is two-stage, following buildGraph. Occurrences whose canonical keys are identical join the same group without scoring. Otherwise a new group is opened and matched, through the hash, against existing people; the best-scoring candidate above the threshold absorbs it, and if none qualifies the group becomes a new person.

Two vetoes constrain every merge. Both come from mustMerge at graph.h:900–924, and neither was present in an earlier version of this port.

A person may not appear twice in one chain. A narrator cannot have transmitted to himself, so if a candidate person already holds an occurrence from the chain under consideration, the merge is refused. Without this veto, narrators merged with their own neighbours, producing self-loops that then surfaced as impossible cycles. Measured before the veto was restored: 76 violations on the first volume.

The group must not span more than r generations. Position within a sanad approximates distance from the source, so two occurrences at very different positions are unlikely to be the same person. The test is on the span of the whole group once the new occurrence joins:

lowest  = min(new_position, group.lowest)
highest = max(new_position, group.highest)
accept  if  highest - lowest <= equality_radius

An earlier version tested instead whether any existing occurrence lay within r, which permits a group to ratchet across generations one hop at a time — position 0 merging with 3, then 3 with 6, then 6 with 9. One node had reached a span of twelve. Measured before the correction: 99 groups exceeded the radius.

6.5 Three levels of node

The paper defines Split as the inverse of Merge: a merged node m(n1…nk) is divided into m1(n1…ni) and m2(ni+1…nk). An operation of that form needs indivisible pieces to redistribute, and that is what the middle node type provides.

ChainNarratorNode
one occurrence in one chain		 → GroupNode
occurrences with an identical key		 → GraphNarratorNode
one person: a set of groups

Figure 6.3 — The node hierarchy. A group's members share one exact written name, so no evidence can separate them; a split therefore redistributes groups, never individual occurrences.

An earlier version of this port collapsed the hierarchy to two levels, leaving the middle type as an unused stub. Split then degenerated into removing one occurrence at a time, which cannot undo a bad fusion and offers no guarantee of progress.

6.6 Cycles and splitting

Narration flows forward in time, so the graph must be acyclic. A cycle means the merge stage fused two different people. The original's response, reproduced here, is to dissolve the offending person into its groups and re-merge them pairwise at a stricter threshold, in three passes with the threshold rising each time. Groups that no longer clear the higher bar emerge as separate people.

Two properties matter. Because groups are atomic, the partition can only become finer, so the procedure terminates. And no edge is ever deleted: a narration relation is evidence, and cycles are resolved by re-partitioning nodes rather than by discarding data. An earlier version of this port deleted an edge when a split failed, and — worse — rebuilt every edge from the chains immediately after the cycle-breaking loop, which silently undid the whole procedure. Measured at the time: 771 back-edges survived a run that reported having broken cycles.

6.7 Generation ranks

Each person is assigned a rank: zero for a source, and otherwise one more than the highest rank among the people they transmitted from. The property that matters is monotonicity — every edge must run from a lower rank to a higher one, or the notion of generation carries no information.

This requires a longest-path relaxation over the acyclic part, computed here in topological order. A shortest-path search, which an earlier version used, breaks monotonicity: a person reachable by both a one-hop and a four-hop route receives rank 1, and the four-hop edge then runs backwards.

7. Stage 3 — The Biography Stage

This is where the cross-document mechanism operates, and it is the part of the original system that had no counterpart in this project before the present work.

7.1 The task

A rijal book is continuous prose. The task is to determine where each entry begins and ends, and which narrator it describes. Doing so from the text alone is the problem the original reports at 41% boundary recall: the automaton can find names, but nothing in the prose tells it whether the next name still belongs to the current entry or begins the next one.

7.2 The same automaton, in biography mode

The original runs the identical state machine with a flag inverted. Five behaviours change; each is traced to the branch that implements it.

7.3 The feedback loop

The widened tolerance in Table 7.1 — 100 words between narrators — looks implausibly permissive on its own. It is not a fixed allowance. The counter it consumes is reset whenever a completed narrator is confirmed against the hadith graph:

where addNarrator succeeds exactly when the narrator matches a node in the graph above the equality threshold. The automaton therefore tolerates up to a hundred words since the last narrator the graph could confirm. 7.4 Looking a narrator up in the graph

The lookup returns not a yes or no but a graded, de-duplicated list of candidate people with their similarities, sorted best first; "is a real narrator" simply means that list is non-empty. The original fills the list and returns the boolean in one call, and the ranked list is what the boundary detector uses to disambiguate.

Two points of care. The threshold is read from the graph's own stored parameters rather than a local default, so that the two stages agree on who counts as the same person — biography names were previously matched at 0.1 against a graph built at 0.5. And the graph's canonical forms are serialised with it, rather than being re-derived from the stored name strings, so that both sides of a comparison are canonicalised identically; otherwise a true match can be missed with no error raised.

7.5 Deriving the boundary

For each person in the graph, the detector collects every position in the mention list where that person occurs, and the positions of the person's neighbours in the graph. An occurrence scores one point for each distinct neighbour that appears within a fixed character distance of it. The best-scoring sets above a threshold are retained, and the span of the winning set — from the earliest to the latest mention it contains — is the entry boundary.

Figure 7.2 — Boundary derivation. The extent of the entry is not read from punctuation or from the entry number; it is the span of the narrators the graph confirms belong together.

Detected entries are then accepted best-first, and any whose span overlaps one already accepted is rejected — a rijal book is a partition, so two entries cannot occupy the same text.

7.6 Annotating a partial graph

Section 4.1 of the paper describes a second mode, and it answers a different question. A scholar interested in a particular topic selects a handful of related narrations, builds a graph from those alone, and asks — for each narrator in it — which biography entries might describe them.

In the original both modes live in one class, separated by two overridden methods: the full-book segmenter considers every narrator and keeps a single non-overlapping answer each, while the base class considers only the nodes the scholar selected and keeps all three top candidates without overlap rejection. The same scoring, asked differently. Segmentation must partition the book; annotation returns a ranked shortlist, because the scholar makes the final call. The paper is explicit that boundary accuracy is not well defined for this mode for exactly that reason.

Why the restricted graph helps is worth stating, since it is the point of the method. In the full graph a prolific narrator has hundreds of neighbours, so the observation that a neighbour appears nearby carries little information. In a graph built from ten related narrations the same narrator has perhaps four, and their co-occurrence within one entry is strong evidence.

8. Stage 4 — The Evaluation Framework

8.1 What has to be measured

Four distinct quantities appear in the original's results, and they require two different kinds of scorer.

8.2 Two-level agreement

The scorer compares two annotations, each a list of main spans — a hadith, or a biography entry — with name spans nested inside them. It reports three things.

Segmentation is the proportion of main spans found at all. Detection is name-level: a predicted name counts as correct if it overlaps a gold name, matched greedily one-to-one so that one prediction cannot claim two gold names. There is no string comparison anywhere in this test; the original's equality function for this purpose is literally interval overlap. Boundary is measured at word granularity: of all the words belonging to gold narrator names, how many fell inside a predicted name span.

The distinction between the second and third is the reason both are reported, and an example makes it concrete. Suppose the gold has three narrators and the system finds two, one of them truncated to its first word:

Table 8.2 — Detection cannot see a bad extent; boundary can.
Measurevaluereading
detection recall0.67two of three names found
boundary recall0.44only four of nine gold words covered

Detection is satisfied by any overlap, so a badly truncated name still counts as found. Only the boundary figure reveals that its extent is wrong. This is why "41% boundary recall" in the original means that 41% of gold narrator words fell inside a predicted span — not that 41% of narrators were found.

Boundary is reported in two variants, as in the original: minimum boundaries score each matched pair independently, while maximum boundaries merge a run of mutually overlapping names into one group before scoring. A gold name split by two predictions therefore contributes twice under the first and once under the second; the difference between the two is itself informative.

The weighting formula is preserved exactly from the original, since simplifying it changes the numbers:

recall     = (common / correct) * correct
precision  = (common / detected) * correct
denominator += correct

The multiplication by correct is a weight. It cancels in recall, producing a true micro-average, and does not cancel in precision, producing a recall-weighted macro-average. One consequence should be stated plainly because it affects interpretation: every contribution is weighted by the gold count, so a prediction overlapping no gold name has weight zero and cannot move precision. Precision here means "of the gold words we claimed, how many were right", not "how much of our output was correct".

8.3 Merge accuracy

Table 1 asks a different question, and the span-based scorer cannot answer it. Given the same occurrences, did we group them into the same people? For each occurrence:

common     = |gold cluster ∩ predicted cluster| - 1
recall     = common / (|gold cluster| - 1)
precision  = common / (|predicted cluster| - 1)

averaged over occurrences, with a singleton counting as trivially correct. This is B-Cubed with the element itself excluded — worth naming, since it is a recognised clustering measure whose properties are documented, rather than a bespoke formula.

It behaves as one would want: under-merging costs recall while leaving precision intact, over-merging costs precision while leaving recall intact. The original reports two rows, and both are reproduced: detected considers only occurrences the system produced, isolating the merge logic, while all includes every gold occurrence, so that a narrator the segmenter never found counts as a cluster of one and all its merges as missed.

8.4 Comparing the name metric

The §5 evaluation is the only one that isolates the distance metric. Everywhere else it is entangled with segmentation and graph construction, so a change to it barely registers. Here it is the entire signal, and each item is a yes-or-no judgement on a pair of names rather than a span, which makes a usable annotation set cheap to produce.

Three conditions are compared: normalised edit distance above 0.75, which is the baseline the original compares against; the structural metric of Section 6.2; and the structural metric with the nisba logic enabled — this project's implementation of the mechanism described in §3.2 of the paper but absent from the released system.

Candidate pairs for annotation are generated from a built graph rather than at random, drawn from names the hash already considers plausible and sampled across score bands. Random pairs are almost all trivially different and would teach the evaluation nothing; the pairs that matter are the ones near the decision boundary:

أحمد                     ||  أحمد بن علي بن محمد بن عبد الله
محمد بن السكين           ||  محمد بن علي بن معمر
موسى بن محمد العجلي      ||  موسى بن محمد بن إسماعيل

8.5 The gold layer

All four scorers consume one canonical annotation format, whatever its origin — imported from the original binaries, produced by hand, or bootstrapped from system output and then corrected. Each annotated unit carries a main span, the name spans within it, and optionally a person label per name, since merge accuracy needs cluster labels rather than offsets.

Two safeguards are built into loading, both taken from the original and both consequential.

Un-reviewed annotation is refused. The original, finding no gold file, would write the current system output as a starting point, print "Correct it before use", and return failure so that no statistics were produced from it. Both halves matter. Here a bootstrapped file is marked un-reviewed and the loader refuses it outright. This is not hypothetical: an earlier component of this project generated its ground truth by calling the same segmenter it was evaluating, so that gold and prediction were the same object and every number derived from it was meaningless. That component has been removed.

Annotation is bound to its text. Each file records a hash of the text it was made against, and loading it against different text is an error rather than a warning. This catches the failure mode in which a change to normalisation shifts every span by a few characters and the numbers merely become quietly worse.

A reader for the original Qt QDataStream format is also provided, so that if the original annotations are recovered they can be scored directly. The layout was taken from the C++ serialisation code rather than inferred: the flat span lists used for names and biography spans, the two-level structure used for hadith, and the pair-judgement map. One detail illustrates why guessing would not have worked — within the two-level format, connector components store their offsets twice, once as 64-bit values inside their own serialisation and again as 32-bit values in the enclosing loop. A parser that missed this would decode every subsequent record into plausible-looking nonsense. The reader is therefore strict: an unexpected type tag raises an error naming the byte position.

9.3 Separating fidelity from improvement

This system differs from the original in two quite different ways, and the distinction has to be maintained if any claim about it is to be defensible.

Some differences are corrections: the gate on name evidence, the handling of honorifics, the two merge vetoes, the monotonic rank. These bring the system closer to what the original source does, and switching them off would make it less faithful, not more. They are always active.

9.4 Testing

The suite comprises 165 tests across eleven files. The governing constraint is that none of them require CAMeL Tools or Wojood to be installed: token features are either derived from the closed-class lexicons and the phrase matcher, or constructed by hand.

This was a deliberate design choice with a practical justification. Loading the models and analysing a book takes minutes; a suite that required them would be run rarely, and regressions would be discovered long after the change that caused them. Constructing features by hand also allows the automaton to be driven into states that real text produces only occasionally, which is where state machines fail.

10.2 Experiments

Experiment 1 — narrator graph accuracy (Table 1). Build the graph from the annotated narrations and score the merge decisions against the person labels, reporting the detected and all rows.

Experiment 2 — the cross-document effect (Table 2). Run the biography automaton twice over the same corpus, identical in every respect except that the graph lookup is supplied in one condition and withheld in the other. Score both with the same scorer. This is the paper's central claim and the design is deliberately minimal: one argument differs.

Experiment 3 — biography detection (Table 3). Score the detected entry spans against the annotated ones, partitioned as the original does into all entries, interesting entries — those naming at least two teachers or students — and those whose subject exists in the graph.

Experiment 4 — the name metric (§5). Score the three conditions of Section 8.4 over the annotated pairs, with a threshold sweep.

Experiment 5 — ablation. Re-run Experiments 2 and 3 with each addition of Table 9.3 disabled in turn, to establish what each contributes.

11. Discussion and Limitations

11.1 Name ambiguity is a limit of the method, not of this implementation

The graph built from the first volume of al-Kafi retains cycles after splitting, and it is worth being precise about why, because the reason is not a defect that further work would remove.

Of the cycles remaining, 651 of 658 run entirely through people whose occurrences share a single exact written name. A group of such occurrences is atomic — no evidence distinguishes its members — so a fusion that lands inside one group cannot be undone by any splitting procedure. The people appearing on the most cycles are precisely the ambiguous short names: أبي بصير, ابن فضال, يونس. These genuinely are several different people sharing one written form, and the name-based metric has no means of separating them.

This is the same phenomenon the original reports, and it is why its Table 1 shows 63% recall: the paper attributes that figure to conservative splitting, which is the other side of the same coin. The honest position is that name evidence alone has a ceiling, and that this system reaches roughly the same ceiling as the original. Section 12.2 suggests what would raise it.

11.2 What the morphological substitution costs

CAMeL Tools and Wojood are trained predominantly on modern standard Arabic; hadith text is classical. This shows in two ways.

Name detection is looser, which Section 5.4 addresses by gating rather than by trusting the tag. The gate recovers the original's behaviour but it substitutes positional context for a curated gazetteer, and a gazetteer would be better evidence.

The geographic categories Sarf provided — Name of Place, City/Town, Country — have no replacement. Nisba detection is currently a shape test: a word beginning ال and ending ي. That is enough to route qualifiers correctly but not to support the reasoning of §3.2, which needs to know that Kufa is in Iraq. The nisba logic implemented here therefore compares qualifier sets rather than geography, and Section 12.2 identifies the lexicon that would complete it.

11.3 Threats to validity

Single annotator. The planned gold will be produced by one person, who is also the system's author — a conflict the original shares. The double-annotated slice of Section 10.1 is the mitigation, and its agreement figure should be reported alongside the results rather than in a footnote.

Corpus scope. The graph is built from al-Kafi and the biographies from al-Khoei. Both are Shia works, so the results speak to that tradition; the original also evaluated on Musnad Ahmad, a Sunni collection with a different structural convention. Nothing in the design assumes one tradition, but that is an argument, not a measurement.

Structural measurements are not accuracy. The figures in Sections 6.8 and 7.7 count invariant violations and structural properties, which need no annotation. They demonstrate that the mechanism operates; they say nothing about whether its output is correct. They are labelled as such wherever they appear.

Parameter selection. The thresholds inherited from the original were tuned on its corpus with its morphological stack. They are exposed as parameters and a sweep is provided, but selecting them on the same annotation used to report results would be circular. If tuning proves necessary, it should use a development slice held out from the reported one.

12. Conclusion and Future Work

12.1 Conclusion

ANGE has been reimplemented in Python end to end, with CAMeL Tools and Wojood in place of Sarf and MySQL. The system segments hadith books into narrations, builds a narrator graph across them, uses that graph to read biography books, and scores every stage with the metrics the original defined.

The work turned out to require more than translation. Reading the released source against the paper established that three described mechanisms were never implemented — the nisba logic in the distance metric, a tunable neighbourhood radius, and the sequential ambiguity constraint — and that two routines contain defects that silently discard results. All three mechanisms are implemented here and kept switchable; both defects are corrected and documented. Independently, the original evaluation data proved to have been excluded from the published repository, which means the reported tables are not reproducible from the published material by anyone; a reader for the original format is provided in case that data is recovered.

Two corrections to this port's own earlier state were substantial enough to report. Accepting the morphological analyser's proper-noun tag without regard to context caused ordinary matn words to be read as narrators; gating it as the original does removed that class of error at its source. Treating the honorific عليه السلام as a narrator accounted for 11.5% of all narrator positions and produced the largest node in the graph.

What remains before the project's central claim can be tested is annotation, not implementation.

12.2 Future work

A narrator gazetteer and a nisba lexicon. The most valuable single addition. A curated list of known narrator names would replace positional context as the evidence behind name detection, and a nisba lexicon mapping adjectives of origin to places would complete the §3.2 metric with the geographic reasoning it describes. Both could be bootstrapped from the graph the system already produces.

Evidence beyond the name. Section 11.1 argues that name-based merging has a ceiling. The evidence that would raise it is already in the data: two occurrences of أبي بصير transmitting from entirely disjoint sets of teachers are probably different people. Using neighbourhood structure as a merge criterion, rather than only as a post-hoc consistency check, is the natural next step.

Iterative refinement. The paper describes repeating the biography pass with progressively lower thresholds, each pass adding narrators the previous missed. The infrastructure for this exists; the loop does not.

The application. The end state the original paper depicts is a scholar selecting a narration, seeing its chain as a graph, and finding each narrator annotated with the credibility assessment drawn from the rijal literature. Every component of that is now present. Assembling them into an interface is a matter of presentation rather than of research, and it is what would make the work useful to the people it is for.
